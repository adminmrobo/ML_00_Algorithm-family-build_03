The Reinforcement House’s Ledger — the reward is the exam

A reinforcement learning agent learns to make the sum of rewards as large as possible. That's all. Whether it does what we really wanted, it has no idea. Write the reward wrong, and the agent finds some odd shortcut where the score goes up but the work never gets done.

In this episode we train Sweepy the cleaning robot with three different rewards. Then we look in turn at three things you must check when measuring reinforcement learning: the mean and spread over several seeds, an unseen layout, and the real goal rather than the reward.

01
Learning curves & seeds

Learning curves and seeds — a curve from one run may be luck

Run the same experiment with several seeds and look at the mean and the spread together

Story

Reinforcement learning involves a lot of luck. At first the agent moves randomly to explore (ε), and the table fills in differently depending on which cell it tries what in first. The number that fixes the starting point of this randomness is the seed.

The graph at right records Sweepy trained for 1,500 episodes each in the same cleaning room with the same reward (reward 2 from chapter 02). Only the seed was changed, five times. The horizontal axis is the episode number; the vertical axis is the sum of rewards in one episode (smoothed over 30 episodes).

The thick line is the mean of the five seeds; the pale band runs between the lowest and highest seed. Pick a single seed and you see only that one line.

This room is small and simple, so the seeds don't differ much. Even so, the same method is dozens of episodes slower or faster depending on the seed. As problems get bigger, as in deep reinforcement learning, this wobble grows much larger — it has been reported that changing just a few seeds can flip the ranking of two methods (Henderson et al., 2018). So we never say “this method learns faster” from a single line.

Reward design lab

Reward design lab — what you reward is what it learns

Same room, same learning method, just three rewards

Story

A 5×5 room has 8 dust cells. Sweepy starts at the charger in the top left and moves for 40 steps. We want just one thing: clear all the dust. But Sweepy can't understand those words. All Sweepy knows is the reward number.

RewardRule
No. 1+1 every time it steps on a dust-patterned cell (even one already cleaned)
No. 2+1 only when a dust cell is cleaned for the first time, 0 on revisits, −0.01 per step
No. 3No. 1 + 5 for returning to the charger (episode ends)

No. 1 looks reasonable — stepping on a dust cell cleans it. But it has a loophole: “stepping on an already-cleaned cell still scores.” Let's train Sweepy and see whether it finds that loophole.

03
What to measure

What to measure — the three columns of the Reinforcement House's ledger

Mean and spread, an unseen layout, and the real goal

Three columns of the ledger

① Mean and spread. Run with several seeds and record the mean and range of cumulative reward together. As in chapter 01, a single curve may be luck.

② Unseen layout. Check whether it works in a new layout, not just the room it learned in. The “unseen room” in the table at right swaps the positions of dust and walls. We see what happens when the table learned in the original room is carried over as is.

③ Measure the real goal separately. A reward is only a proxy metric that turns what we want into a number. For cleaning, measure the real goal separately from the reward, e.g. “share of cells cleaned”. If the two diverge, redesign the reward. In real life, an agent in a boat-racing game was reported to circle endlessly around a spot with score items instead of heading for the finish line (Clark & Amodei, 2016).

Field check ④

In reinforcement learning, measure the real goal separately from the reward, run several seeds, and report the mean and spread together.

    About the data, illustrations, and concept of this page

    • Data and calculations. The cleaning room (5×5, 8 dust cells, 2 wall cells, 40 steps per episode) and the three rewards are examples set up for explanation (fixed random seeds 11–15). All training and evaluation are computed live by this page in your browser with Q-Learning (α 0.3, γ 0.95, ε decayed from 1 to 0.05, 1,500 episodes). Evaluation is one episode run as learned, without exploration.
    • Source material. The structure of the family tree is based on the Machine Learning Algorithm Family Tree infographic (Pooja Pawar). No images were copied; only the relationships between houses, branches, and algorithms were used as a reference, and everything was redrawn.
    • Illustrations. The characters and scenes were drawn for this series. The NPCs and House crests are drawn pixel by pixel by the page itself.
    • The "Don" concept. Only the general mood of the crime-family genre is borrowed. No characters, lines, logos, or music from any specific film are used, and there is no violence. "House" is simply a metaphor for a group of algorithms.
    • Characters. Money-Robo is the mascot of a service run by the author. The Doctor, the Assistant, the Fruit Fly Researcher, and the NPCs do not represent real people. Money and investment talk in the dialogue is just a verbal habit, not investment advice.
    • Language. This series was written in Korean and translated into English, Chinese, Japanese, Uzbek, and Russian. Comments and output in the Colab code are left in the original Korean.
