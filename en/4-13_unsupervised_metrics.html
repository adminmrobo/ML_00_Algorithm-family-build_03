The Unsupervised House’s Ledger — measuring without answers, and the trap of pictures

Unsupervised learning has no answers to check against. Yet the ledger must still be kept. Instead of answers, we ask a single question: is each group tight inside, and are the groups far apart?

Turning that question into numbers gives the elbow and the silhouette. And one more thing: a 2-D picture such as t-SNE looks crisp, but it is not a number. With one setting changed, the same data can look like four groups or like six.

01
The elbow

The elbow — is one more group worth it?

Find where the within-cluster sum of squares bends

Story

K-Means assigns each point to the nearest group center. Square the distance between every point and its own center and add them all up: that is the within-cluster sum of squares. The smaller it is, the tighter the groups.

The catch is that this value always goes down as the number of groups k goes up. Split 240 customers into 240 groups and it hits 0. So instead of choosing the smallest value, we choose the bend (the elbow), where the drop suddenly gets small.

On the curve at right, the dashed line joins the first and last points. The k farthest from this line is the bend. Move the slider to change k, and the group colors in the picture below change too.

02
Silhouette

Silhouette — close to my group, far from the next?

One bar from −1 to 1 for every point

How it is computed

For each customer we measure two distances.

  • a = mean distance to customers in the same group (smaller is better)
  • b = mean distance to customers in the nearest other group (larger is better)

The silhouette value is (b − a) ÷ (the larger of a and b). Close to 1 means the point sits well in its place, near 0 means it is on a border, and negative means the neighboring group is closer.

Averaging the silhouette values of all customers gives the mean silhouette score. Vary k from 2 to 10 and pick the k with the highest score.

Try moving the slider above 4. Force one group to split and red negative bars appear.

The t-SNE trap

The t-SNE trap — every mirror shows a different number of groups

The same 8-D customers, four maps with just one setting changed

Story

t-SNE spreads the 8-dimensional customers out onto a 2-D map. It places points so that each point's close neighbors stay close on the map too (4-8). That is why the groups look so crisp.

But change the setting that decides “roughly how many neighbors to pay attention to” — the number of neighbors (perplexity) — and the picture changes. Look at few neighbors and even the small sub-clumps inside a group break away. Clump sizes and the gaps between clumps are not faithful copies of the original distances either.

The “clumps counted by eye in the picture” below links points closer than 6% of the map width and counts the pieces (DBSCAN). It imitates a person counting by looking at the picture.

Choose a t-SNE map

The four maps were precomputed with scikit-learn TSNE: number of neighbors (perplexity) 3·8·30·80, init="pca", random_state=0, max_iter=1000. The customer data is the same set of 8-D points; the elbow, silhouette and clump counts are computed live by this page in your browser.

    • Data and calculations.
